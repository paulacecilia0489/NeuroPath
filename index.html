    Why you write your own question

    • Professors receive many generic emails. A specific question about their paper is what gets answered.
    • NeuroPath only supplies the structure. The question must be yours (12+ words), and it is checked for ties to the lab's actual work.
    • Before you can copy, download or send a draft, you confirm four best-practice checks. Contact each lab once, and wait 5–7 business days before a single follow-up.

    These are coaching prompts, not enforced limits. Per-lab monthly caps are part of the planned opt-in version.

    Sending guide: timing and staying out of spam

    Rules of thumb that many mentors and career offices share. They are not guarantees, and professors differ.

    Tip: use Schedule send. You will probably finish writing at night, but a message that arrives at 11 PM looks automated and sinks under morning mail. Write whenever you like, then schedule it for Tuesday–Thursday, 8–10 AM in the professor's time zone. Gmail (arrow next to Send), Outlook and Apple Mail all have it.

    When to send

    • Best days: Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday. Mondays are buried in weekend mail, and Friday or weekend emails often sit unread.
    • Best hours: 8–10 AM their time, before lectures and meetings. Avoid evenings and the middle of the night.
    • Best months: January–February for summer, April–May for fall, October–early November for spring. Avoid finals weeks and the first two weeks of a term.

    Avoiding the spam folder

    • Plain text. No all-caps subject, no stacked exclamation marks, no sales words (“guarantee”, “click here”, “act now”).
    • Send from your real full name. Keep any attachment (CV, paper) a compact PDF, ideally under 2–3 MB, or just link it.
    • A few tailored emails per day, spread over the week. Never send the same message to several people in one department.
    • NeuroPath sends nothing itself. Your own email app sends it, so your normal account settings apply.

    Following up

    • Wait 5–7 business days, then send one short, polite reply on the same thread. Do not start a new email. After one follow-up, move on.

      About

      Real neuroengineering shouldn't need a lab key.

      The problem

      Most high school students never touch real biosignals. Equipment is expensive, labs rarely say whether they welcome students, and nobody explains what a first project looks like. Students without mentors fall furthest behind.

      What NeuroPath does

      • Signal Lab: a hands-on EMG pipeline (band-pass, notch, rectify, envelope, threshold) with a scored challenge and a virtual gripper. Use simulated signals or upload a recording from your own sensor.
      • Find a lab: matches your interests and skills to labs, then helps you write a specific email. You must write your own question, so the tool cannot be used to mass-send templates.
      • No-lab path: pointers to open datasets so you can keep going on your own.

      Privacy

      NeuroPath has no accounts and no server. Uploaded files are processed in your browser and never uploaded. The only thing saved, on your own device, is which checks you completed.

      What is real and what is a demo

      • The signal-processing code is real and the same math used in research tools. The simulated muscle is simulated, built to include realistic problems (60 Hz hum, drift, cable bumps).
      • The lab directory currently contains demo entries only. They are clearly marked and cannot generate emails. Real labs are added only after they agree to be listed.

      What comes next

      A two-sided version where labs set a monthly cap on student inquiries, outcomes are tracked anonymously, and teachers can run a class cohort. It needs real lab partners and a school pilot first.

      Limits

      This is an educational tool. The gripper is a visualization, not a medical device. A simple threshold gives only open and closed, while real prosthetic control often needs graded force and pattern recognition.
